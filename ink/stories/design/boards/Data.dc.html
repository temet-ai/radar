Rows, meters and
charts in cells.

Most of radar is lists of things in states. A row has to say what it is, where it stands and whether it wants you in one line. Ink lets the numbers behind those rows draw as shapes: smooth meters, real charts, diffs that point at the word that changed.

Trees & row states

Connectors show depth. A row is in one of five states, shown by its ground alone, so its colours keep their meaning. A group is marked by the heading shade; the coloured stripe groups carry today down their left edge goes.

One tree, every part

Anything with children is this tree, in every part: GitLab's groups and repos, Jira's epics, Slack's threads, Git's worktrees. Same fold signal (tree lines, and ⋯ n on a folded row, as GUIDE 2.12 says; no chevrons), same keys, same five row states, same heading shade. A part gives the tree its rows; it never draws its own.

Pipelines as graphs

A GitLab pipeline is a graph of jobs, not a list. Stages are columns, jobs are boxes bordered in their state, needs are lines. The failed job is the one the eye lands on; the chosen job is the selection shade, and ⏎ opens its log.

Narrow screens fall back to the stage list, with only failed or running jobs open.

{{s.t}}

Diagrams

A mermaid block in a description, an MR or a Slack message is drawn, not shown as source. Boxes in accent.muted, decisions in warning, the end in success, edges muted with their labels in italic. Flowcharts first; sequence diagrams draw the same way with lifelines.

source flips back to the text.

The editor & markdown

Writing a description, a comment or a reply opens the editor workspace: the source on the left with its syntax in the slots, the rendered page on the right, both scrolling together. The same markdown renderer draws every description and message in radar, so what you preview is what everyone sees.

Narrow screens show one side; ctrl-p flips.
